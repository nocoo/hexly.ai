## Composition and camera

The group fills roughly 80–84% of the square with breathing room around the flame tips and wing tips. The campfire carries about two-thirds of the visual weight; the insect is substantial enough to read as the identity.

Use one coherent, slightly elevated three-quarter camera. The firefly approaches the fire from the upper right toward the lower left: head closest to the flame, thorax behind the head, luminous abdomen trailing farther up and right. The visible front of the head turns toward the fire and remains legible to the camera. Two modest eyes belong to that anterior head surface with natural foreshortening, not the rear head or thorax. Antennae and body orientation reinforce the same direction.

A foreground flame lobe may cover a small inner wing/root area to establish depth. Preserve the insect's head, eye pair, luminous abdomen, and at least 85% of its silhouette. The fire remains clear in front. Avoid a detached tiny insect, oversized face, large anatomical leg details, or a symmetrical camera-facing mascot unrelated to the fire.

## Two intentional materials

The fire is a premium three-dimensional campfire render: rounded curling tongues with continuous warm shading, overlapping volume, pale hot interior planes, orange outer tongues, and small red ember seams at the base. It must look like fire, not a faceted crystal, flat triangular sheet, pixel stack, or plastic ornament. Three short warm-brown split logs provide a compact tactile base, with restrained grain and charred edges. No landscape, rock bed, smoke cloud, or loose debris.

The firefly uses the family drawing language: connected irregular flat polygons across its head, thorax, abdomen, and broad opaque wing planes. Bronze/amber body, champagne wings, restrained teal and violet shadow facets, and an internally bright honey-gold abdomen preserve the recent color direction. Keep insect expression neutral and anatomy discreet.

The fire is the primary foreground mass and the animal's external interest point. Its relationship to the approaching insect carries the image, rather than an artificial eye arrangement or decorative confetti.
